Tempo

A vision agent that reads what is happening inside the pots and conducts the order, so every dish on a ticket is ready together.
OpenCV AI Competition 2026 · powered by AWSPaths: Agentic Vision with OpenCV 5Team: Hyesoo Kim (solo)OpenCV 5.0.0 · Python 3.12 · AWS Graviton
In one paragraph. Kitchens already time what happens after water boils; nothing tells them when it will boil. That unknown — minutes that vary with burner, pot, volume and tap temperature — is why dishes of one table finish at different times and why pots are left unattended until they boil over. Tempo puts a camera above each burner. OpenCV 5 turns the liquid surface into stage events (shimmer → simmer → rolling boil) and a foam-overflow forecast; a first-order heating model turns those events into a time-to-boil estimate; an agent re-plans every open ticket so its dishes finish together, drives a screen at every station (prep board, burner line, single burners, pass, whole-kitchen board) and lowers the heat before foam overflows. In a kitchen simulator with 120 services per condition, Tempo cut the time cooked food waits by 66% in quiet service and 38% in a rush, and reduced boil-overs from 7.5 to 0.2 per 100 dishes. On photo-realistic simulated footage it detected the rolling boil within 1.1 s in 8/8 pots and warned 13 s before foam overflow with no false alarms.
Honesty note. All camera results in this report come from simulated footage generated by our own renderer, labelled "Simulated footage" in every frame. Kitchen results come from a simulator whose assumptions are listed in §6. Real pot video was used only for a by-eye sanity check on eight short stock clips (§6.5), which exposed two failure modes we report rather than hide; §8 lists exactly what remains unproven.

1 · Problem and users

A ticket for one table often mixes dishes with different heat-up and cook times: a stew, a ramen, an egg soup. A plain kitchen display fires every dish when the order arrives; the long dish defines the table and the short ones finish early and cool, go soggy or get re-heated. Experienced cooks stagger starts by feel, but the stagger depends on how long each pot will take to boil, which no timer covers. Boiling also invites leaving the pot: unattended cooking is the leading cause of restaurant fires (cooking equipment starts ≈61% of fires in US eating and drinking places, NFPA).

Users: small and mid-size kitchens that boil — ramen and noodle shops, pasta, soups and stews, school and hospital catering, delivery kitchens — usually with one or two cooks running four to six burners and staff who may not share a language. Existing systems focus elsewhere: kitchen camera systems verify order assembly (e.g. Agot AI) or track workers' actions for station displays (US 11,562,569); jam-detection cameras alert but do not plan. None reads the state of the liquid or schedules a table around it. Tempo never films people.

2 · What the system does

  • Prep board screen — what to cut for which ticket and how much ("T6 kimchi stew: kimchi 150 g, tofu ½ block in 2 cm cubes"), and what is next.
  • Burner-line strip — a long screen above a row of burners: one column per burner with countdown to boil or end of cook, a fill showing progress, and a blinking do-now (LOAD / ADD / PLATE).
  • Single-burner and pass screens, LED rings — same colour language; any phone or tablet pairs by QR code. LED rings (ESP32 firmware included) are simulated in the demo.
  • Whole-kitchen board — tickets on a rail with "all ready in m:ss", all burners, prep, pass, the camera, and why the plan changed.

Physics-informed prediction

A pot heats as a first-order system, C dT/dt = P − h(T − Tenv). Temperature is invisible, but each visible stage appears at a characteristic temperature. Given the time a stage first appears, a ratio fit anchored at the start gives the time to boil; later stages carry more weight; the estimate never predicts the past. Before any evidence the burner's learned prior is used, and each finished heat-up updates that burner's power estimate. Stage temperatures are calibrated per camera setup from a few clips that run to a boil (fractions of heat-up time; leave-one-out in all evaluations).

4 · The agent

Decide. Every open ticket is split into prep, burner and plate steps. A forward pass assigns each waiting dish to the burner on which it finishes first (with hysteresis so screens do not reshuffle); each ticket's target is its latest finish; dishes are then started as late as possible without delaying the next dish that needs the same burner. Re-planning is triggered by new orders and by camera stage events, and never reads future information. The agent also keeps the cook's hands free when a boil is predicted within the duration of a task it would otherwise start.

Act. Prompts on station screens with a chime; lowering heat when foam is forecast to overflow (the only automatic action — safe and reversible); SNS alerts. Gas shut-off is out of scope (certified hardware). Trace. Every decision is logged with its visual cause, e.g. "Camera: B2 pasta reached simmer, boil now predicted at 35:39 → T5 ramen start +16 s, T5 omelette start +40 s".

5 · AWS deployment

One CloudFormation template (deploy/cloudformation.yaml) creates an EC2 t4g.small (Graviton, arm64) instance running the Docker image, an encrypted private S3 bucket with 30-day expiry for results and traces, an SNS topic for safety alerts, a CloudWatch log group with 14-day retention, and a least-privilege IAM role (S3 put/get on its own bucket, SNS publish on its own topic, logs). No SSH: administration via SSM; IMDSv2 required; the container runs as a non-root user with a health check. deploy/deploy.sh deploys from AWS CloudShell in one command; teardown.sh removes everything. A public endpoint serves the live board, station screens, the LED simulation and POST /api/vision, which runs the perception on an uploaded pot video.

Reading the numbers honestly. Most of the reduction in waiting comes from planning tickets to finish together at all; the camera adds a further 8–17 s (quiet) and 13–26 s (rush) per dish versus the camera-less schedule (95 % bootstrap CIs) and almost eliminates boil-overs. Predicting the boil adds little over merely detecting it for synchronisation; its value is in the countdowns staff act on and in keeping the cook free. In a rush, synchronising costs about 14 % longer order-to-table time; we plan a "speed first" mode rather than hide this.

6.3 Failure cases and what we fixed

  • Two minutes out, the camera knows little. Before the first visible stage the estimate equals the burner prior (±33 s vs ±35 s).
  • Camera shake hid the boil. On the first realistic clip the boil went undetected because jitter inflated the motion baseline; subtracting the median flow fixed it.
  • Fixed foam brightness missed foam under warm light; the threshold is now relative to each pot's liquid.
  • Compression shifts stages. H.264 moves the shimmer stage earlier (~62 °C instead of ~72 °C); calibration absorbs it, which is why calibration clips must come from the deployed camera.
  • An evaluation leak we found ourselves. An early simulator let planners read a pot's future boil time; all results above were produced after the fix and the regression test guards it.
  • Renderer artefact. In the evaluation clips, noodles late in the clip were drawn beyond the pot rim (after all measured events); the demo clip uses the corrected renderer.

6.5 Sanity check on real footage

Stock clips of real pots (Pixabay / Pexels, free licence; 5–38 s each) start mid-process, so there is no cold baseline and no ground-truth timeline. We therefore test only the absolute part of the boil rule (flow ≥ 1.5 px and moving area ≥ 4 %, thresholds unchanged from the simulated evaluation), with the pot outline set by one tap as at installation. 7 · Responsible use

  • Pots, not people. Cameras are aimed at burners; no faces, workers or audio are processed or stored; vision uploads are deleted after analysis and results expire from S3 after 30 days.
  • Humans stay in control. Every prompt can be ignored; the only automatic action is lowering heat, which is reversible and safety-positive; gas shut-off is deliberately excluded.
  • No overclaiming. Simulated footage is labelled in the frame and in this report; simulator assumptions are listed; trade-offs are reported.
  • Accessibility. Colour, fill and blinking carry meaning together with large text (2–3 m legibility); on station screens, reduced-motion mode replaces blinking with a frame; a chime and vibration accompany new actions.
  • Development disclosure. The project was built with the help of an AI coding assistant; all results are reproducible with the scripts listed below. The assistant is not part of the submitted agent.

8 · Limits and next steps

  • Record full cold-to-boil real footage (a phone on a stand over home and restaurant burners) and report it next to the simulated results; add hand/utensil masking (6.5).
  • Integrate a real POS feed; add a "speed first" planning mode for rushes.
  • Benchmark Cloud-Optimized OpenCV (COOL) on Graviton for multi-camera kitchens (not claimed here).
  • Projected prompts on the cutting board; induction hobs with an API for closed-loop heat control.
